Gameplay

Each round is one day with two phases where players act: morning and afternoon. Between them, the game runs things automatically: once at midday and again overnight. Design sketch, not built yet

Morning players trade

Players buy and sell on the market to get the inputs their businesses need for the day. Whatever a business holds when morning ends is what it produces with at midday.

Midday automatic

  1. Businesses turn their inputs into products
  2. Markets reprice, now that stockpiles have changed
  3. Something else? not decided

Afternoon players act

Players play action cards that decide how the market moves overnight, for example triggering a pandemic.

  • Set up new businesses not decided
  • Move around the map not decided

Overnight automatic

  1. Markets reprice, based on the action cards played this afternoon
  2. Loan repayments are charged
  3. Bond yields are paid out
  4. Futures expire and settle
  5. Workers are paid
  6. Some open orders get filled not decided

Market dynamics

Every commodity's price moves with how much of it players are holding. When stockpiles are full the price falls; when the stuff is scarce it climbs.

Price = Base × Scarcity × Demand × Inflation
Scarcity
(s₀ ÷ s)e. s is the share of all players' combined stockpile space holding this commodity. s₀ is its balanced share, where the price equals the base price. e sets how sharply the price reacts. Shares are held between s₀ ÷ 10 and s₀ × 10, so prices can't run away.
Demand
Units all factories need ÷ units they normally need. 1.0 is normal. This will come from the factories in play; for now you can set it by hand.
Inflation
(1 + rate)round. Everything creeps up over time.
Base
Set by hand for raw resources and wastes. Every made good gets its base from its recipe: the base cost of its inputs, minus any by-products it can sell, plus any disposal fees, × 1.3 markup, ÷ the units it makes.
Waste
Wastes with no use have a negative price: a disposal fee. Their fee grows as more piles up, so their scarcity term flips to (s ÷ s₀)e.

Example: wheat, base 6, s₀ = 50%, e = 1. At 50% full: 6 × (50 ÷ 50) = 6. At 10% full: 6 × (50 ÷ 10) = 30.

What each number means

Price

What one unit sells for on the market right now. Every player buys and sells at this price.

Base

The price when everything is normal: stockpiles balanced, demand normal, no inflation yet. Think of it as the commodity's "fair value".

Raw resources and wastes have a base set by hand (wheat is 6). Made goods work theirs out from their recipe, so a smartphone's base comes from the bases of its circuit board, battery and glass.

Markup 1.3

How much a factory adds on top of what its inputs cost. At 1.3, a business that buys its inputs at base prices sells its output for 30% more. That gap is the profit that makes running a factory worthwhile.

Share, s

How much of all players' stockpile space, added together, is filled with this commodity right now.

If all players together have room for 1,000 crates and 100 of them hold wheat, wheat's share is 10%.

Balanced share, s₀

The share at which the price equals the base: the "healthy" amount of this commodity to have in the economy. Bulk goods like wheat have a large balanced share; rare, high-end goods like wind turbines have a tiny one.

The balanced shares of all 50 commodities add up to 100%, so a perfectly balanced market has every stockpile full.

Scarcity

How much the price is pushed up or down by supply: (s₀ ÷ s)e. Above 1 means scarce, so the price is higher; below 1 means plentiful, so it's lower.

Wheat with s₀ 50% and s 10%: 50 ÷ 10 = 5, so wheat costs 5 times its base.

Elasticity, e

How strongly the price reacts when stock runs short or piles up. Higher means wilder swings.

At half the balanced share: e = 1 doubles the price (×2.0), e = 1.4 gives ×2.6, and e = 1.6 gives ×3.0.

Staples like wheat and water use 1.0. Specialised goods with no substitute, like chips or motors, use up to 1.6, because when you need one there's nothing else you can buy.

Price limits ×10

For the price, a share never counts as more than 10 times below or above balance. This stops a commodity that runs out completely from costing infinity, or a flooded one from becoming free.

Wheat at 1% is priced as if it were at 5%: 6 × 10 = 60, not 300.

Demand

How badly factories need this commodity compared with normal. 1.0 is normal, 1.5 means they want 50% more, and 0.5 means half as much. It multiplies the price directly.

It will be worked out from how many factories are running and what their recipes use. For now you can type it in.

Inflation

Prices creep up over time, the same for everything: (1 + rate)round.

At 2% a round: ×1.02 after round 1, ×1.22 after round 10, and ×2.69 after round 50.

Round

One game day (morning, midday, afternoon and overnight). Inflation is applied once per round.

Waste fees

Tailings and chemical waste have no use, so their price is negative: you pay to get rid of them. Their scarcity term is flipped, (s ÷ s₀)e, so the more that piles up across all players, the higher the fee gets. Demand doesn't apply, since nobody wants them.

Slag, fly ash and sawdust can be recycled, so they're priced like any other good.

vs base

How far the current price is from the base, in percent. Orange means above base and blue means below.

Stockpile space used

All 50 current shares added together. It can't go over 100%, because players can't hold more than their space allows. It shows red if your edits push it past 100%.

Broker dynamics

The money side of the game: what it costs to borrow, how much you can borrow, and three ways to bet on prices. Every rate is per round (one game day). Change any input to see the maths update.

1 · Central bank
Base rate = neutral + 1.5 × (inflation − target)

The central bank sets the base rate: what it costs to borrow from the government. Every other rate in the game is the base rate plus something on top. The bank's job is to keep inflation near its target.

Neutral
2.5%. The base rate when inflation is exactly on target.
Target
2% inflation a round, the rate of price rises the bank is happy with.
× 1.5
How hard the bank reacts. Inflation 1% over target raises the base rate by 1.5%. Reacting by more than 1-for-1 is what real central banks do (the "Taylor rule"), so borrowing actually gets more expensive in real terms.
Limits
Never below 0.25% or above 10%. The bank only changes the rate every 5 rounds, so players can see it coming.
2 · Government loans
Credit limit = 50% of stockpile + 60% of businesses + 90% of bonds

You borrow from the government against what you own. Your businesses are the main collateral. If you can't pay, the bank takes your stuff.

Haircut
Each kind of collateral only counts for part of its value, because its price might fall before the bank can sell it. Stockpiles swing most (50% counts), businesses are steadier (60%), and government bonds are nearly cash (90%).
LTV
Loan-to-value: loan ÷ everything you own. 30% LTV means you've borrowed 30% of your total worth.
Loan rate
base + 0.5% + 3% × LTV. The more you've borrowed relative to what you own, the riskier you are and the more you pay.
Overnight
Each overnight you're charged interest (loan × rate) plus 5% of the loan as repayment, so a loan is paid off in about 20 rounds.
Margin call
If prices fall and your loan ends up above your limit, you must pay down the difference by next morning. If you can't, the bank sells your stockpile at 20% under market, then your businesses, until you're back under.
3 · Leverage
Position = your money × leverage

Buy more of a commodity than you can afford, with the broker lending you the rest. Gains and losses are multiplied, and so is the risk.

Leverage
1× to 5×. At 3×, 1,000 of your own money buys 3,000 worth of goods; the broker lends the other 2,000.
Interest
base + 2% a round on the borrowed part, for as long as you hold.
Your stake
What the goods are worth now − what you borrowed − interest. A 10% price rise at 3× is roughly a 30% gain on your money; a 10% fall is a 30% loss.
Liquidation
Your stake must stay at least 25% of the position. If the price falls far enough that it doesn't, the broker sells everything to get its money back. The liquidation price = entry × (leverage − 1) ÷ (leverage × 0.75).
4 · Futures
Futures price = spot × (1 + base + storage)rounds

A contract to buy (long) or sell (short) a commodity at a fixed price on a later round. Farmers use them to lock in a price for their harvest; traders use them to bet on where prices are going.

Spot
Today's market price.
Why higher
The futures price is above spot because the seller has to fund and store the goods until then: base rate plus 0.5% a round of storage. This is called the "cost of carry".
Margin
You don't pay the full price up front, just 10% as a deposit. Every overnight your gain or loss so far is added to or taken from it. Below 7.5%, you must top it up.
Settlement
Up to 10 rounds ahead. On the day, no goods change hands: you're just paid (or pay) the difference. Long: (price then − futures price) × units. Short: the reverse.
5 · Government bonds
Bond price = coupons left + 100, each discounted at today's yield

Lend the government 100 for 5, 10 or 20 rounds. It pays you a fixed amount every overnight (the coupon) and gives your 100 back at the end. Safe, unless you need to sell early after rates have risen.

Coupon
Fixed on the day you buy: base rate then + 0.05% for every round of the bond's length. Longer bonds pay a little more for tying your money up.
Yield
What a new bond with the same rounds left would pay today: base now + 0.05% × rounds left.
Price
You can sell a bond to other players before it ends. Its fair price is everything it still pays, each payment discounted by (1 + yield) for every round you'd wait for it.
Rates ↑ price ↓
When the base rate rises, new bonds pay more, so old bonds paying less are worth less. Long bonds swing the most. This is the main risk of holding bonds.
Glossary

Round

One game day. All rates here are per round. Interest, loan repayments, coupons and futures margin changes all happen overnight.

Inflation

How fast prices rise on average each round. The same number drives the inflation term on the Market dynamics tab.

Real rate

Base rate minus inflation. If it's negative, holding goods beats holding cash, and borrowing to buy is tempting. If it's positive, cash and bonds look better.

Collateral

Things you own that the lender can take and sell if you don't pay: your stockpile, your businesses and your bonds.

Premium / spread

The extra a lender charges on top of the base rate for the risk of lending to you. Government loans: 0.5% + 3% × LTV. Broker leverage: a flat 2%.

Long / short

Long means you profit when the price rises. Short means you profit when it falls.

Liquidation

The broker or bank selling your position or property without asking, to get its money back.

Discounting

Money later is worth less than money now, because money now could be earning interest. 100 paid in 2 rounds at 3% is worth 100 ÷ 1.03² = 94.26 today.

Face value

The 100 a bond pays back on its final round.
