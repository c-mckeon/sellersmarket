Gameplay

Sellers Market is a trading game over rounds. You score points by delivering secret contracts; most points wins, and cash only breaks ties. Nothing has a set price: every price comes from what players bid and ask. Raw materials are each found in one region of the map, so to make what your contracts ask for you have to trade with players far away.

Day players order

Everyone enters their orders in secret: buy and sell orders, contracts to deliver, one contract to throw away if you want, and one of these: move your pawn to a neighbouring territory, or build a business where you are. Then they all go through at once. If two players move into the same territory, neither gets in.

Night automatic

  1. The sealed auction: each good trades at one price (see Market dynamics)
  2. Every business produces 2 of its product: mines dig, factories turn inputs into products
  3. Goods at the end of their life go off
  4. Delivered and thrown-away contracts are replaced

Starting out

  • cash. It's the only money there will ever be: it's used for trading and nothing else.
  • Two free businesses where you start: a mine (or farm, or well) and the factory that uses its output, e.g. an iron mine and a blast furnace.
  • secret contracts.
  • A stockpile of rows, one unit in each. The setup screen spreads players out, each with a different region's mine.

Contracts and points

  • A contract asks for 2 or 3 goods in set amounts, about raw materials' worth in all, and never more than units. Deliver it whole, any time you trade, from anything you own (your stockpile or your businesses' stock; goods in sell orders don't count).
  • Contracts ask for these made goods: . Each item has a chance of being a raw material instead: .
  • They lean towards goods made far from where you started, so you'll need other players.
  • Points = raw materials in it × × (1 + for each step its goods come from) × (1 + for each processing step). Made goods pay more than raw ones.
  • You can throw one contract away a round; new ones arrive overnight.
  • Each good shows how many other players' contracts want it: a count only, never who or how many.

Businesses and production

  • Once a round, at night after the auction, each business runs and makes 2 of its product. Mines, pits, farms and water works need nothing; factories need every input, from your stockpile.
  • Stand-ins: a factory short of an input can use another good instead, e.g. . Goods a contract of yours asks for are never used as stand-ins.
  • What your businesses make goes into your stockpile: rows, one unit in each, first in at the top. It's first in, first out: the oldest units are used, sold and delivered first, and when it overflows the oldest are pushed out. Goods in your sell orders keep their rows; buy orders keep rows free for what they could bring in.

Goods go off

  • Every unit remembers when it was made and goes off at the end of its life: . Each stockpile row counts down to it.
  • Goods are used, sold and delivered oldest first, and keep their age in sell orders and when bought, so holding goods back has a cost.

Building

  • Building is free: one business a round, instead of moving, in the territory you're in.
  • Each territory allows only certain businesses: the mines, pits and farms for what's found there, the factories that process it, and bakeries and concrete plants in big cities. Click a territory to see its list.

Market dynamics

There is no price list. Every price comes from what players bid and ask, matched in one sealed auction each night. Only players trade: there are no outside buyers or sellers.

Orders

  • A buy order (bid) or sell order (ask): a good, a price and a quantity. Prices are whole numbers from 1 to .
  • A bid locks price × quantity of your cash. An ask takes the goods from your businesses first, then your stockpile; they keep ageing while on offer. Nothing can be promised twice.
  • Orders stay on the book until they fill or you cancel them. You can't place an order that would trade with one of your own.
  • Everyone's new orders are secret until the auction; the book you see is what's left from earlier auctions.

The auction

Each good on its own. Of all the whole-number prices, it picks:

  1. the one at which the most units change hands;
  2. if several tie, the one that leaves the fewest units unmatched;
  3. if buyers are still left over at all of them, the highest (sellers left over: the lowest);
  4. otherwise the one nearest the last traded price (never traded: the middle).

Everyone who trades gets that one price: a buyer who bid more gets the difference back. Better prices fill first; orders at exactly the clearing price share what's left in proportion to their size.

Example. You bid 5 for 1 water, someone else bids 2 for 1, and a seller asks 1 for 1. Any price from 3 to 5 sells that one unit to you and not to them, so you get it, at 3: just enough to beat the other bid. If the seller had 3 to sell, you'd both get one, at 1.

Rule 4 is why bidding the maximum "to be sure" is safe: one very high bid can't drag the price up on its own.

What you can see

  • The book, anonymous: price levels and quantities, never who. Each good's page draws it as a depth chart, asks above and bids below, with the spread between them.
  • Trades: the last price and volume, a price history, and a quote board of every active good that flashes yellow when one trades. You can replay how the book moved over the last few rounds, auction by auction.
  • Cost basis of a made good: its raw materials at their last traded prices. Raw materials have no price until they first trade; the market has to find it.
  • Demand: how many other players' contracts want each good (a count only), and Opportunities: goods others are bidding for that nobody sells, with the margin over cost basis.
  • Who owns which businesses, and so who makes and who could use each good. Contracts and stockpiles stay private.

Why it works this way

  • Sealed and simultaneous, like every other move: nobody can wait to see your order and jump in front of it.
  • One price for everyone: you can bid what a good is really worth to you and still pay only what it takes to win it.
  • Prices only from players, and contracts that need goods from other regions, so the way to progress is to make what others need and trade for what you need.

Broker dynamics

The money side of the game, kept simple for now. Every rate is per round (one game day). Change any input to see the maths update.

1 · Central bank
Base rate = inflation + 0.5%

The central bank sets the base rate: what it costs to borrow from the government. Every other rate is the base rate plus a bit.

Inflation
How fast prices are rising each round (2% to start).
+ 0.5%
Keeps the rate just above inflation, so saving is always worth a little.
Reviews
Changed every 5 rounds. Never below 0.25% or above 10%.
2 · Government loans
Borrowing limit = 50% of what you own

Borrow from the government against your stockpile, your businesses and your bonds. If you can't pay, the bank takes them.

Limit
Half the value of everything you own. Own 7,500 of stuff, borrow up to 3,750.
Rate
Base rate + 1% a round.
Overnight
You pay the interest plus 5% of the loan back, so a loan is gone in about 20 rounds.
Margin call
If prices fall and your loan ends up over your limit, pay the difference by next morning, or the bank sells your things 20% under market.
3 · Leverage
Profit = (your money × leverage) × price change − interest

Buy more than you can afford, with the broker lending the rest. Gains and losses are multiplied.

Leverage
1× to 5×. At 3×, 1,000 of yours buys 3,000 worth; the broker lends 2,000.
Interest
Base rate + 2% a round on the borrowed part.
Sold out
Once you've lost 75% of your own money, the broker sells everything. At 3× that happens after a 25% price fall (75% ÷ 3).
4 · Futures
Futures price = today's price × (1 + base rate × rounds)

Agree a price today to buy (long) or sell (short) on a later round. Lock in a price you need, or bet on where it's going.

Why higher
The seller could have sold today and earned interest on the money instead, so the later price includes that interest.
Deposit
Put down 10% of the contract up front.
Settling
Up to 10 rounds ahead. No goods move: you're paid (or pay) the difference. Long: (price on the day − futures price) × units. Short: the other way round.
5 · Government bonds
Each overnight you're paid 100 × the rate you locked in

Lend the government 100 for 5, 10 or 20 rounds. You lock in the base rate on the day you buy, get paid it every overnight, and get your 100 back at the end.

Selling early
100 + (your rate − today's base rate) × 100 × rounds left.
The catch
If rates rise after you buy, new bonds pay more than yours, so yours sells for less than 100. Hold to the end and you still get 100.
6 · Sector ETFs
ETF price = 100 × average of (price ÷ base price)

One fund for each sector. A unit tracks every good in that sector at once, so you can bet on "construction" without picking cement or concrete, and without filling your stockpile.

Starts at 100
When every good in the sector is at its base price, the ETF is 100. If they're on average 20% above base, it's 120.
Live
Uses the current prices from the Market dynamics tab. Shuffle the stockpiles there and these move too.
Fee
0.5% when you buy. Units take no stockpile space.
Wastes
Left out, since their prices are fees.
Glossary

Round

One game day. All rates here are per round. Interest, loan repayments and bond payments happen overnight.

Inflation

How fast prices rise on average each round. The same number drives prices on the Market dynamics tab.

Collateral

What the bank can take if you don't pay: your stockpile, businesses and bonds.

Margin call

A demand to pay down a loan, or top up a deposit, because prices moved against you.

Long / short

Long means you profit when the price rises. Short means you profit when it falls.

ETF

Exchange-traded fund: one thing you can buy that tracks a whole basket of goods.

Action cards

Cards are played in the afternoon and change the market overnight. There are 50 so far, covering commodities, businesses, sectors, whole markets, credit and defaults, and new government regulations. Designed, not wired in yet

How cards will plug into the maths

1Cards change numbers, not formulas

Every card is a short list of effects. Each effect says which existing variable it nudges (its hook), what it applies to, by how much, and for how long. The pricing, production and broker formulas stay exactly as they are; they just read the nudged numbers.

Drought
  • Demand Water ×2 3 rounds
  • Raw supply Water ×0.5 3 rounds

This keeps the game fair and easy to balance. A new card never needs new maths, just a new list of effects, and any card's impact can be worked out with the calculators on the other tabs.

2Where the effects go

The formulas we already have gain a slot for active card effects. They're shown in orange; with no cards in play, each one is ×1 or +0 and nothing changes.

Business output = recipe output × card output, running costs × card costs, value × card value

3When they happen

Cards are played face down in the afternoon. Overnight they're revealed and settled in a fixed order, so the result never depends on who played first:

  1. Counters: cards like Insurance payout or Bailout cancel what they target.
  2. Regulations switch on (or off, once we add repeal cards).
  3. One-off shocks: stockpiles destroyed or added, loans called in, contracts torn up.
  4. Markets reprice, using every effect still active.
  5. Money: the base rate moves, haircuts change (which can trigger margin calls), then interest, coupons and futures settle.
  6. Timers tick: each effect loses a round, and finished ones are removed.

Next morning, players trade at the new prices.

4Stacking and limits

Effects on the same hook and the same target combine: multipliers multiply (two ×1.5 demand cards make ×2.25), additions add. To stop a pile of cards breaking the economy, the combined multiplier on any one thing is held between ×0.25 and ×4, and the base rate still stays between 0.25% and 10%.

5How long they last

Each effect lasts once (it happens overnight and is done), for a set number of rounds, or until repealed (mostly regulations). Effects stay at full strength until they run out. If markets feel too jumpy when they switch off, we can make effects fade out over their last few rounds instead.

6Why play a card: you know first

The player holding a card knows what's coming before anyone else. Holding Drought? Buy water in the morning, play the card that afternoon, and sell the next morning once the price jumps. Or take a futures position on it. Planning a day ahead around your hand is the heart of the strategy. Other players' defence is to read the market and keep counter cards ready.

7Still to decide

  • How players get cards: a few drawn each morning, bought, or earned?
  • Whether playing a card costs money, and whether rare cards cost more.
  • How many cards one player can play in an afternoon.
  • Whether cards are revealed with the player's name, or anonymously.
  • How often each rarity turns up (for example common 60%, uncommon 30%, rare 10%).

How to read this

Table

A grid of one kind of thing: one row per player, per business, per trade.

Primary key (PK)

The column, or columns together, that identify each row. No two rows can share it. players.id 1 is always the same player.

Foreign key (→)

A column holding another table's id: businesses.owner_id = 1 means "owned by player 1". Click any → column, or any value in one, to jump there.

Design data vs game data

Design data (commodities, recipes, territories, cards) is the rulebook and is the same in every game. Everything else belongs to one game through game_id, so many games can run side by side.

One row per thing

Lists live in their own tables. A recipe's inputs aren't squeezed into one column; each input is a row in recipe_inputs. That makes questions like "which businesses use coal?" easy to answer.

History

market_prices and trades are only ever added to, never changed, so we can replay a game and draw price charts.
