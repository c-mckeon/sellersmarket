Gameplay

Each round is one day with two phases where players act: morning and afternoon. Between them, the game runs things automatically: once at midday and again overnight. Every phase is simultaneous: all players submit their orders in secret, then everything happens at once. If two players try to move into the same territory, neither gets in. Morning players trade

Players buy and sell on the market to get the inputs their businesses need for the day. Whatever a business holds when morning ends is what it produces with at midday.

Territories

Each of the 60 territories has its own character, based on real geography: what's in the ground, whether it's on the coast, and how expensive it is to build there. Mines and farms can only go where their resource is; most factories can go anywhere.

Build cost = base cost × territory cost level × (1 + 25% × businesses already there)
Base cost
Set for each kind of business. A farm is 100, a gold mine 260, an offshore oil rig 450. A factory costs 150, 250, 400 or 700, depending on how advanced its product is.
Cost level
Land and wages differ. London is ×2.0 and Paris ×1.8; Ukraine is ×0.7 and North Africa ×0.6.
Crowding
Every business already in a territory, anyone's, makes the next one 25% dearer. The first is ×1, the fifth is ×2. Popular spots fill up and get expensive, which spreads players out.
Richness
Mines, farms and wells produce more where the deposit is richer: ★ ×1, ★★ ×1.5, ★★★ ×2. Swedish iron mines out-produce Scottish ones.
Where
Extraction only where the resource is. Oil refineries and plastics plants need the coast or a local oil field. Every other factory can go on any land. At sea you can only build oil rigs, where there's offshore oil.

In the game, more settings apply (tuned by simulation): every cost is ×1.5, each business you already own makes your next one 30% dearer, every business pays wages overnight of 3% of its cost, and mines, farms and wells make 20% more than listed here.

Example: a coal mine (base 140) in the Ruhr (×1.2) with 2 businesses already there: 140 × 1.2 × 1.5 = 252. It makes 4 × 2 = 8 coal a round, since the Ruhr's coal is ★★★.

Market dynamics

Every commodity's price moves with how much of it players are holding. When stockpiles are full the price falls; when the stuff is scarce it climbs.

Price = Base × Scarcity × Demand × Inflation
Scarcity
(s₀ ÷ s)e. s is the share of all players' combined stockpile space holding this commodity. s₀ is its balanced share, where the price equals the base price. e sets how sharply the price reacts. Shares are held between s₀ ÷ 10 and s₀ × 10, so prices can't run away.
Demand
Units all factories need ÷ units they normally need. 1.0 is normal. This will come from the factories in play; for now you can set it by hand.
Inflation
(1 + rate)round. Everything creeps up over time.
Base
Set by hand for raw resources and wastes. Every made good gets its base from its recipe: the base cost of its inputs, minus any by-products it can sell, plus any disposal fees, plus 20% of the factory's base build cost as value added, ÷ the units it makes.
Waste
Wastes with no use have a negative price: a disposal fee. Their fee grows as more piles up, so their scarcity term flips to (s ÷ s₀)e.

Example: wheat, base 6, s₀ = 4.09%, e = 1. When wheat is 4.09% of all stock: 6 × (4.09 ÷ 4.09) = 6. When it's down to 0.82%: 6 × (4.09 ÷ 0.82) = 30. (Wheat's balanced share used to be 50%; simulations showed that made farms unbeatable, so every good's share is now set so a balanced stockpile holds about equal value of each.)

What each number means

Price

What one unit sells for on the market right now. Every player buys and sells at this price.

Base

The price when everything is normal: stockpiles balanced, demand normal, no inflation yet. Think of it as the commodity's "fair value".

Raw resources and wastes have a base set by hand (wheat is 6). Made goods work theirs out from their recipe, so a smartphone's base comes from the bases of its circuit board, battery and glass.

Value added 20%

What a factory adds on top of its inputs. Each run adds value worth 20% of the factory's base build cost, so a flour mill (base cost 150) adds 30 to the value of its flour. This means every kind of business earns roughly the same share of its cost at base prices, and differences come from location, competition and cards.

Share, s

How much of all players' stockpile space, added together, is filled with this commodity right now.

If all players together have room for 1,000 crates and 100 of them hold wheat, wheat's share is 10%.

Balanced share, s₀

The share at which the price equals the base: the "healthy" amount of this commodity to have in the economy. Cheap bulk goods like water and sand have a large balanced share; rare, high-end goods like wind turbines have a tiny one.

The balanced shares of all 50 commodities add up to 100%, so a perfectly balanced market has every stockpile full.

Scarcity

How much the price is pushed up or down by supply: (s₀ ÷ s)e. Above 1 means scarce, so the price is higher; below 1 means plentiful, so it's lower.

Wheat with s₀ 4.09% and s 0.82%: 4.09 ÷ 0.82 = 5, so wheat costs 5 times its base.

Elasticity, e

How strongly the price reacts when stock runs short or piles up. Higher means wilder swings.

At half the balanced share: e = 1 doubles the price (×2.0), e = 1.4 gives ×2.6, and e = 1.6 gives ×3.0.

Staples like wheat and water use 1.0. Specialised goods with no substitute, like chips or motors, use up to 1.6, because when you need one there's nothing else you can buy.

Price limits ×10

For the price, a share never counts as more than 10 times below or above balance. This stops a commodity that runs out completely from costing infinity, or a flooded one from becoming free.

Wheat (s₀ 4.09%) at 0.1% is priced as if it were at 0.41%: 6 × 10 = 60, not 245.

Demand

How badly factories need this commodity compared with normal. 1.0 is normal, 1.5 means they want 50% more, and 0.5 means half as much. It multiplies the price directly.

It will be worked out from how many factories are running and what their recipes use. For now you can type it in.

Inflation

Prices creep up over time, the same for everything: (1 + rate)round.

At 2% a round: ×1.02 after round 1, ×1.22 after round 10, and ×2.69 after round 50.

Round

One game day (morning, midday, afternoon and overnight). Inflation is applied once per round.

Waste fees

Tailings and chemical waste have no use, so their price is negative: you pay to get rid of them. Their scarcity term is flipped, (s ÷ s₀)e, so the more that piles up across all players, the higher the fee gets. Demand doesn't apply, since nobody wants them.

Slag, fly ash and sawdust can be recycled, so they're priced like any other good.

vs base

How far the current price is from the base, in percent. Orange means above base and blue means below.

Stockpile space used

All 50 current shares added together. It can't go over 100%, because players can't hold more than their space allows. It shows red if your edits push it past 100%.

Broker dynamics

The money side of the game, kept simple for now. Every rate is per round (one game day). Change any input to see the maths update.

1 · Central bank
Base rate = inflation + 0.5%

The central bank sets the base rate: what it costs to borrow from the government. Every other rate is the base rate plus a bit.

Inflation
How fast prices are rising each round (2% to start).
+ 0.5%
Keeps the rate just above inflation, so saving is always worth a little.
Reviews
Changed every 5 rounds. Never below 0.25% or above 10%.
2 · Government loans
Borrowing limit = 50% of what you own

Borrow from the government against your stockpile, your businesses and your bonds. If you can't pay, the bank takes them.

Limit
Half the value of everything you own. Own 7,500 of stuff, borrow up to 3,750.
Rate
Base rate + 1% a round.
Overnight
You pay the interest plus 5% of the loan back, so a loan is gone in about 20 rounds.
Margin call
If prices fall and your loan ends up over your limit, pay the difference by next morning, or the bank sells your things 20% under market.
3 · Leverage
Profit = (your money × leverage) × price change − interest

Buy more than you can afford, with the broker lending the rest. Gains and losses are multiplied.

Leverage
1× to 5×. At 3×, 1,000 of yours buys 3,000 worth; the broker lends 2,000.
Interest
Base rate + 2% a round on the borrowed part.
Sold out
Once you've lost 75% of your own money, the broker sells everything. At 3× that happens after a 25% price fall (75% ÷ 3).
4 · Futures
Futures price = today's price × (1 + base rate × rounds)

Agree a price today to buy (long) or sell (short) on a later round. Lock in a price you need, or bet on where it's going.

Why higher
The seller could have sold today and earned interest on the money instead, so the later price includes that interest.
Deposit
Put down 10% of the contract up front.
Settling
Up to 10 rounds ahead. No goods move: you're paid (or pay) the difference. Long: (price on the day − futures price) × units. Short: the other way round.
5 · Government bonds
Each overnight you're paid 100 × the rate you locked in

Lend the government 100 for 5, 10 or 20 rounds. You lock in the base rate on the day you buy, get paid it every overnight, and get your 100 back at the end.

Selling early
100 + (your rate − today's base rate) × 100 × rounds left.
The catch
If rates rise after you buy, new bonds pay more than yours, so yours sells for less than 100. Hold to the end and you still get 100.
6 · Sector ETFs
ETF price = 100 × average of (price ÷ base price)

One fund for each sector. A unit tracks every good in that sector at once, so you can bet on "construction" without picking cement or concrete, and without filling your stockpile.

Starts at 100
When every good in the sector is at its base price, the ETF is 100. If they're on average 20% above base, it's 120.
Live
Uses the current prices from the Market dynamics tab. Shuffle the stockpiles there and these move too.
Fee
0.5% when you buy. Units take no stockpile space.
Wastes
Left out, since their prices are fees.
Glossary

Round

One game day. All rates here are per round. Interest, loan repayments and bond payments happen overnight.

Inflation

How fast prices rise on average each round. The same number drives prices on the Market dynamics tab.

Collateral

What the bank can take if you don't pay: your stockpile, businesses and bonds.

Margin call

A demand to pay down a loan, or top up a deposit, because prices moved against you.

Long / short

Long means you profit when the price rises. Short means you profit when it falls.

ETF

Exchange-traded fund: one thing you can buy that tracks a whole basket of goods.

Action cards

Cards are played in the afternoon and change the market overnight. There are 50 so far, covering commodities, businesses, sectors, whole markets, credit and defaults, and new government regulations. Designed, not wired in yet

How cards will plug into the maths

1Cards change numbers, not formulas

Every card is a short list of effects. Each effect says which existing variable it nudges (its hook), what it applies to, by how much, and for how long. The pricing, production and broker formulas stay exactly as they are; they just read the nudged numbers.

Drought
  • Demand Water ×2 3 rounds
  • Raw supply Water ×0.5 3 rounds

This keeps the game fair and easy to balance. A new card never needs new maths, just a new list of effects, and any card's impact can be worked out with the calculators on the other tabs.

2Where the effects go

The formulas we already have gain a slot for active card effects. They're shown in orange; with no cards in play, each one is ×1 or +0 and nothing changes.

Price = Base × (s₀ ÷ s)e × card e × Demand × card demand × Inflation(rate + card inflation), then held under any price cap
Share s = stock after stockpile shocks, ÷ total space. New raw units each morning = normal supply × card supply
Business output = recipe output × card output, running costs × card costs, value × card value
Base rate = central bank rate + card changes. Borrowing limit = 50% − card changes of what you own. Loan rate = base + 1% + card change. New bond rate = base + card change

3When they happen

Cards are played face down in the afternoon. Overnight they're revealed and settled in a fixed order, so the result never depends on who played first:

  1. Counters: cards like Insurance payout or Bailout cancel what they target.
  2. Regulations switch on (or off, once we add repeal cards).
  3. One-off shocks: stockpiles destroyed or added, loans called in, contracts torn up.
  4. Markets reprice, using every effect still active.
  5. Money: the base rate moves, haircuts change (which can trigger margin calls), then interest, coupons and futures settle.
  6. Timers tick: each effect loses a round, and finished ones are removed.

Next morning, players trade at the new prices.

4Stacking and limits

Effects on the same hook and the same target combine: multipliers multiply (two ×1.5 demand cards make ×2.25), additions add. To stop a pile of cards breaking the economy, the combined multiplier on any one thing is held between ×0.25 and ×4, and the base rate still stays between 0.25% and 10%.

5How long they last

Each effect lasts once (it happens overnight and is done), for a set number of rounds, or until repealed (mostly regulations). Effects stay at full strength until they run out. If markets feel too jumpy when they switch off, we can make effects fade out over their last few rounds instead.

6Why play a card: you know first

The player holding a card knows what's coming before anyone else. Holding Drought? Buy water in the morning, play the card that afternoon, and sell the next morning once the price jumps. Or take a futures position on it. Planning a day ahead around your hand is the heart of the strategy. Other players' defence is to read the market and keep counter cards ready.

7Still to decide

  • How players get cards: a few drawn each morning, bought, or earned?
  • Whether playing a card costs money, and whether rare cards cost more.
  • How many cards one player can play in an afternoon.
  • Whether cards are revealed with the player's name, or anonymously.
  • How often each rarity turns up (for example common 60%, uncommon 30%, rare 10%).

How to read this

Table

A grid of one kind of thing: one row per player, per business, per trade.

Primary key (PK)

The column, or columns together, that identify each row. No two rows can share it. players.id 1 is always the same player.

Foreign key (→)

A column holding another table's id: businesses.owner_id = 1 means "owned by player 1". Click any → column, or any value in one, to jump there.

Design data vs game data

Design data (commodities, recipes, territories, cards) is the rulebook and is the same in every game. Everything else belongs to one game through game_id, so many games can run side by side.

One row per thing

Lists live in their own tables. A recipe's inputs aren't squeezed into one column; each input is a row in recipe_inputs. That makes questions like "which businesses use coal?" easy to answer.

History

market_prices and trades are only ever added to, never changed, so we can replay a game and draw price charts.
