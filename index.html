Gameplay

Sellers Market is a trading game over rounds. You earn money by winning contracts (the lowest bid gets the job) and delivering them in time; most money at the end wins. Nothing has a set price: every price comes from what players bid and ask. Raw materials are each found in one region of the map, so to make what your contracts ask for you have to trade with players far away.

Day players order

Everyone enters their orders in secret: buy and sell orders, contracts to deliver, and one of these: move your pawn to a neighbouring territory, or build a business where you are. Then they all go through at once. If two players move into the same territory, neither gets in.

Night automatic

  1. The sealed auction: each good trades at one price (see Market dynamics)
  2. Every business produces 2 of its product: mines dig, factories turn inputs into products
  3. Goods at the end of their life go off
  4. Delivered contracts pay out and are replaced, and so are stale ones

Starting out

  • cash. It's the only money there will ever be: it's used for trading and for building businesses.
  • Two free businesses where you start: a mine (or farm, or well) and the factory that uses its output, e.g. an iron mine and a blast furnace.
  • A stockpile of rows, one unit in each. The setup screen spreads players out, each with a different region's mine.

Contracts and money

  • There are always contracts on the board. Each asks for 1 to 4 different goods, a few of each, so some are simple and some complex.
  • Bid what you'd want to be paid for one. Bids are sealed until the night, when the lowest bid so far is shown (never who made it); a new bid of yours must be lower than your last. The client pays at most a cap, shown on the board: raw materials in it × × (1 + for each processing step).
  • After rounds the lowest bid wins (a tie: the earlier bid). You can hold contracts at once; if you're full, the next lowest bidder gets it. No bids: it's replaced.
  • The winner has rounds to deliver it, from their stockpile (goods in sell orders don't count), and is paid their bid. Miss the deadline and you pay legal fees: .

  • Competition sets the prices: a contract only you can make goes for close to its cap; one everyone can make gets bid down.

Businesses and production

  • Once a round, at night after the auction, each business runs and makes 2 of its product. Mines, pits, farms and water works need nothing; factories need every input, from your stockpile.
  • Waste: sawmills, blast furnaces and steelworks also make 1 slag or sawdust a round. Nothing uses it and it goes off after 3 rounds, but it takes up stockpile rows and can't just be thrown away. On the market it runs backwards: you pay someone to take it away, or get paid to take someone else's.
  • Selling a business: any time you order, sell one of your businesses back for its cost price. It disappears; it doesn't use your move or build.
  • Stand-ins: a factory short of an input can use another good instead, e.g. . Goods a contract of yours asks for are never used as stand-ins.
  • What your businesses make goes into your stockpile: rows, one unit in each, first in at the top. It's first in, first out: the oldest units are used, sold and delivered first, and when it overflows the oldest are pushed out. Goods in your sell orders keep their rows; buy orders keep rows free for what they could bring in.

Goods go off

  • Every unit remembers when it was made and goes off at the end of its life: . Each stockpile row counts down to it.
  • Goods are used, sold and delivered oldest first, and keep their age in sell orders and when bought, so holding goods back has a cost.

Building

  • Building costs 120 to 170 cash, depending on the business (mines, pits and farms are cheapest). One business a round, instead of moving, in the territory you're in. Cash in your buy orders can't be spent on it.
  • Each territory allows only certain businesses: the mines, pits and farms for what's found there, the factories that process it, and bakeries and concrete plants in big cities. Click a territory to see its list.

Market dynamics

There is no price list. Every price comes from what players bid and ask, matched in one sealed auction each night. Only players trade: there are no outside buyers or sellers.

Orders

  • A buy order (bid) or sell order (ask): a good, a price and a quantity. Prices are whole numbers from 1 to .
  • A bid locks price × quantity of your cash. An ask takes the goods from your businesses first, then your stockpile; they keep ageing while on offer. Nothing can be promised twice.
  • Orders stay on the book until they fill or you cancel them. You can't place an order that would trade with one of your own.
  • Everyone's new orders are secret until the auction; the book you see is what's left from earlier auctions.

The auction

Each good on its own. Of all the whole-number prices, it picks:

  1. the one at which the most units change hands;
  2. if several tie, the one that leaves the fewest units unmatched;
  3. if buyers are still left over at all of them, the highest (sellers left over: the lowest);
  4. otherwise the one nearest the last traded price (never traded: the middle).

Everyone who trades gets that one price: a buyer who bid more gets the difference back. Better prices fill first; orders at exactly the clearing price share what's left in proportion to their size.

Example. You bid 5 for 1 water, someone else bids 2 for 1, and a seller asks 1 for 1. Any price from 3 to 5 sells that one unit to you and not to them, so you get it, at 3: just enough to beat the other bid. If the seller had 3 to sell, you'd both get one, at 1.

Rule 4 is why bidding the maximum "to be sure" is safe: one very high bid can't drag the price up on its own.

Waste runs backwards

  • Slag and sawdust are worth less than nothing. A sell order says "I'll pay up to this much a unit for someone to take it"; it locks the waste and the fee. A buy order says "pay me at least this much a unit and I'll take it"; it locks nothing but stockpile room.
  • They trade when someone will pay at least what someone else asks; everyone gets the one clearing fee. On the order book the takers' fees sit above the payers', the other way round from normal goods.

What you can see

  • The book, anonymous: price levels and quantities, never who. Each good's page draws it on a price scale running bottom to top: asks as red bars to the right of a centre line, bids as green bars to the left, the spread shaded between them, faint grey blobs where orders sat in recent rounds, and a dot for the last sale.
  • Trades: the last price and volume, a price history, and a quote board: a small order book for each active good (price bottom to top, asks right, bids left) that flashes yellow when it trades and grows into the full view when clicked. You can replay how the book moved over the last few rounds, auction by auction.
  • Cost basis of a made good: its raw materials at their last traded prices. Raw materials have no price until they first trade; the market has to find it.
  • Demand: how many open contracts ask for each good, and Opportunities: goods others are bidding for that nobody sells, with the margin over cost basis.
  • Who owns which businesses, and so who makes and who could use each good. The contracts are public; stockpiles stay private.

Why it works this way

  • Sealed and simultaneous, like every other move: nobody can wait to see your order and jump in front of it.
  • One price for everyone: you can bid what a good is really worth to you and still pay only what it takes to win it.
  • Prices only from players, and contracts that need goods from other regions, so the way to progress is to make what others need and trade for what you need.

How to read this

Table

A grid of one kind of thing: one row per player, per business, per trade.

Primary key (PK)

The column, or columns together, that identify each row. No two rows can share it. players.id 1 is always the same player.

Foreign key (→)

A column holding another table's id: businesses.owner_id = 1 means "owned by player 1". Click any → column, or any value in one, to jump there.

Design data vs game data

Design data (commodities, recipes, territories, cards) is the rulebook and is the same in every game. Everything else belongs to one game through game_id, so many games can run side by side.

One row per thing

Lists live in their own tables. A recipe's inputs aren't squeezed into one column; each input is a row in recipe_inputs. That makes questions like "which businesses use coal?" easy to answer.

History

market_prices and trades are only ever added to, never changed, so we can replay a game and draw price charts.
